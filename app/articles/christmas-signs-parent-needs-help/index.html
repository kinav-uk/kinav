Visiting at Christmas: signs your parent may need more help

Christmas is often the first time in months that families spend a few days with an older parent, and it's when many notice that things have changed. Here's what to look out for, how to bring it up gently, and where to get help. This is general information, not medical advice: for any worry about their health or memory, speak to their GP.

Around the house

  • Post and bills piling up, unopened letters, or reminders and final demands.
  • Little food in the fridge, or food well past its date.
  • The house less clean or tidy than usual, or washing building up.
  • The heating off, or the house feeling cold.
  • Burnt pans, or the cooker or taps left on.
  • New dents or scrapes on the car.

Looking after themselves

  • Wearing the same clothes, or finding washing and dressing harder.
  • Clothes looser than before, or eating less.
  • Medicines not being taken as planned, or packs left unopened.
  • Less steady on their feet, holding on to furniture, or bruises they can't explain.
  • Not going out as much, or dropping hobbies and friends.

Memory and everyday tasks

  • Asking the same question or telling the same story more than usual.
  • Getting muddled about dates, or missing appointments.
  • Struggling with the phone, the TV remote or a meal they've always cooked.
  • Worrying about money, or new people “helping” with it. Older people are often targeted by scams.

When to talk about it

One change on its own may mean nothing. A few together, or a change from how they were last year, is worth talking about. Confusion that comes on suddenly, over hours or days, is different: get medical help the same day from the GP or NHS 111.

How to bring it up

  • Choose a calm moment, not the middle of Christmas dinner.
  • Start with what matters to them, such as staying in their own home or keeping their independence.
  • Talk about one thing at a time, and ask how they feel about it.
  • Listen more than you talk. They may already be worried.
  • You don't have to sort everything in one conversation. Agree one small next step.
  • Talk to brothers and sisters too, and agree who will do what. Kinav's “Who does what” sheet can help.

What you can do next

  • Write down what you noticed, with examples and dates. It helps the GP and the council.
  • If you're worried about their health or memory, suggest a GP appointment, and ask if you can go with them.
  • Ask the council for a free care needs assessment. Anyone can ask, whatever their savings. Kinav can write the letter for you.
  • Ask the council about a free occupational therapy home assessment for equipment such as grab rails. Equipment and small changes under £1,000 should be free in England.
  • Check Attendance Allowance. It's up to £114.60 a week, and savings don't count. Use Kinav's Benefits calculator.
  • Talk about a Lasting Power of Attorney. It can only be made while they can still understand it.
  • If they need help at home, find care rated Good or Outstanding by CQC near them with Kinav.

If you live far away

  • Swap numbers with a neighbour or friend who can look in.
  • Agree regular times for a phone or video call.
  • Ask about a key safe, so help can get in if needed.
  • Ask the council about a personal alarm, which lets them call for help at the press of a button.
  • Tell their GP surgery who to contact, and ask them to record your parent's consent for you to discuss their care.
