Day centres: how they help your parent, and you

A day centre can be one of the simplest ways to make caring more manageable. Your parent gets company, activities and a hot meal, and you get a regular, reliable break. Here's what they offer, how to find one, and how to help the first visits go well.

What happens at a day centre

Day centres are usually run by councils or local charities such as Age UK. Most are open on set days, often from mid-morning to mid-afternoon.

  • Company and conversation, with people of a similar age.
  • Activities such as music and singing, quizzes, gentle exercise, games, and arts and crafts.
  • A hot lunch and drinks through the day.
  • Transport to and from home, often by an accessible minibus. There may be a charge.
  • At some centres, extras such as hairdressing, foot care or help with bathing.
  • Some centres specialise in dementia care.

How it helps your parent

Many older people become lonely and less active when getting out is hard. A regular day out gives the week some shape and something to look forward to.

Being with others, joining in activities and having a proper meal can help them stay connected and more independent at home. Staff get to know them and can let you know if they notice anything has changed.

How it helps you

Caring with no breaks is exhausting, and it's one of the main reasons carers become unwell or give up work. A day centre gives you hours you can count on each week.

  • Go to work, or work without interruptions.
  • Go to your own appointments, or catch up on sleep.
  • See friends, or simply have time to yourself.
  • Know they're safe, fed and in company while you're apart.
  • Keep caring for longer, because you're not running on empty.

How to find one

Many day centres, especially those run or funded by the council, only take people referred by a social worker. So the best first step is to ask the council's adult social care team for a needs assessment, and ask about day centres at it.

Other centres let you contact them directly and pay privately, and some offer a free taster day. Local charities such as Age UK, Alzheimer's Society and carers' centres often know what's available nearby.

Paying for it

Costs vary from place to place, and some charity-run sessions are free or low cost. If the council arranges it after a needs assessment, it may help pay, depending on a financial assessment of your parent's money, not yours.

If they pay themselves, benefits such as Attendance Allowance can be used towards the cost. Use Kinav's Benefits calculator to check what they may be missing.

Questions to ask a day centre

  • Which days and hours are you open, and how much does it cost?
  • Is transport included, and what time are pick-up and drop-off?
  • What activities are there, and can they try a taster day?
  • What help can staff give, for example with the toilet, eating or medicines?
  • Are staff trained in dementia care, if that's needed?
  • How do you meet dietary needs?
  • What happens if they become unwell during the day, and how will you contact us?

Making the first visits go well

It's normal for a parent to be unsure at first, and some say no straight away. A gentle approach helps.

  • Visit together first, or try a taster day.
  • Call it by what they'll enjoy, such as a lunch club or a music group, rather than “care”.
  • Start with a shorter day or one day a week, then build up.
  • Tell staff about their routine, likes and dislikes. For someone with dementia, a This is me form from Alzheimer's Society helps.
  • Label glasses, hearing aids, coats and bags.
  • Give it a few weeks. Many people come to look forward to their day.

Respite: a longer break

If you need more than a day, ask the council about respite care: a short stay in a care home, or a carer staying at home, so you can rest or go away. Ask for a carer's assessment for yourself too. It's free, and it looks at what would help you keep going.
